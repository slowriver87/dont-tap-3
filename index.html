A two-minute test of how long your attention holds.

How to play

Numbers flash in the big box, one about every second. Tap the box for every number, except 3. When you see a 3, hold still. There are 90 numbers, with 10 threes mixed in.

Each number shows briefly, then a blank marker covers it, so you have to read each one. On a computer, use the space bar. To stop partway, hold the Quit button for one second, or press Esc twice.

The science behind this game

What it measures

This game measures sustained attention: how well you stay on task when the task is dull and your hands can run on habit. It's modeled on the Sustained Attention to Response Task, a lab test where people respond to every digit except one [1]. Your score is the share of 3s you held back on.

How this compares to the lab. The lab test counts "commission errors," the times people respond to the digit they should skip. This game reports the opposite, the share they stopped on, because it's easier to read. Lab versions use many more digits per run. This one uses 90 digits with 10 threes, so each round is short and noisier.

What the research says

It tracks everyday slips. In the original study, people's errors on this task were linked to their own reports, and those of people who knew them, of everyday attention failures. People's responses also sped up before errors [1].

Slips come with a wandering mind. When people were asked about their thoughts during a task like this, mind wandering came with activity in the brain's default network and its executive system. Mind wandering was linked to performance errors, and the effect was strongest when people weren't aware their minds had wandered [2].

Minds wander a lot. A study that sampled people's thoughts at random moments during their day found their minds were wandering in 46.9% of samples. People were less happy when their minds were wandering [3].

The cost of drifting. A review of mind-wandering research describes how attention shifts away from the main task, leading to failures in performing it [4].

A phone buzz costs you, even unchecked. Phone notifications alone disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, even when people didn't touch their phones [5].

Adult norms

In one study of university students doing the standard visual version, people tapped on about 40% to 45% of the digits they should have skipped, across different conditions [6]. That means they stopped on roughly 55% to 60%. That range is our summary of that one study, not an official norm. It used a longer test with more targets, so treat the comparison as approximate.

What's still debated

Are attention spans shrinking? That's unsettled. You may have heard that people now have an 8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish's. We found no peer-reviewed study behind that number, so treat it as unsupported. A review of a related popular claim, that students can only focus for 10 to 15 minutes in a lecture, found the commonly cited sources don't support it [7].

Limits of this game

One round is noisy. With only 10 threes, each slip moves the score 10 points. In our simulation of 20,000 rounds, one round landed within about ±30 points of a player's true level 95% of the time. Pooling 10 rounds narrows that to about ±10 points. The scoring showed no lean high or low.

Phones add noise. Screen refresh and touch timing add a little jitter to tap speeds, which matters most for the speed-before-slips number.

Careful players can game it. Tapping slowly cuts slips but causes more missed numbers. That's why the game also reports misses.
