Why your thumb tapped the 3

Tapping becomes automatic fast. After a few dozen taps, your thumb runs on its own rhythm. Your brain stops checking each number closely, because almost every number means tap.

Then a 3 shows up. Stopping takes a deliberate "wait, no." If your mind has drifted, that check comes too late and your thumb is already down. That's a slip.

You speed up before you slip. Researchers noticed that people tap faster in the few seconds before a slip. Faster tapping is a sign of autopilot, so the chart often shows a run of quick taps right before a red mark.

What this can't tell you. There are only 10 threes, so one slip changes your score by 10%. Phone screens also add a little timing noise. Lab versions use over 200 numbers. Your history across several runs says more than one game.

The science

  • The original test. This is the Sustained Attention to Response Task. Its creators found that people who slipped more on the 3 also reported more everyday attention failures, like walking into a room and forgetting why. They also found people sped up right before slips. Robertson and colleagues, 1997
  • Slips track mind-wandering. When people were stopped during this task and asked what they were thinking about, slips were more common right after their minds had wandered. Brain scans showed the brain's daydreaming network active before errors. Christoff and colleagues, 2009
  • Our minds wander a lot. A study that pinged people's phones at random moments found their minds were wandering in about 47% of samples. People were also less happy when their minds wandered. Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010
  • Attention fades with time. During World War II, radar operators missed more signals as their watch went on. A clock-watching test built to study this showed accuracy dropping within the first half hour. Mackworth, 1948
  • A phone buzz costs you, even unchecked. People doing this exact task made more slips after their phone got a notification, even when they didn't look at it. Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert, 2015
  • The goldfish claim is a myth. You may have heard that human attention span is now 8 seconds, shorter than a goldfish's. It spread from a 2015 marketing report. When reporters looked for the research behind the number, they couldn't find any.

What's still debated. Whether attention spans are actually shrinking is unsettled. Researcher Gloria Mark tracked how long office workers stayed on one screen before switching: about two and a half minutes in 2004, and around 47 seconds in more recent studies. That shows our switching habits have changed. Whether our underlying ability to focus has changed is a separate question, and the evidence there is much weaker.

Based on Robertson and colleagues 1997, Christoff and colleagues 2009, and Stothart and colleagues 2015.
